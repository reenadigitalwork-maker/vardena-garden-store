THE VARDENA FIELD GUIDE

Growing well in a small space, through sun, heat and monsoon

Light, heat, rain and limited room shape every balcony garden in India. These five guides take them one at a time, in plain language, with steps you can try, mistakes worth avoiding and a short takeaway to keep. Start at the beginning, or jump to the season that is worrying you most.

Guide 1 · Sunlight

Guide 1

Understanding sunlight on your balcony

Sunlight decides more than anything else which plants will do well where you live. A tomato in a dim corner and a fern in a blazing west-facing balcony will both struggle, however carefully you water them. A little time spent learning your light saves a lot of disappointment later.

Map your light in one day

  1. Pick a clear day. Heavy cloud will make your space look darker than it usually is.
  2. Check at set times. Look at your balcony or windowsill in the morning, around midday, in the afternoon and in the early evening. A photo from the same spot each time makes comparing easy.
  3. Note what you see. For each spot, mark whether it is in direct sun, bright shade or deep shade at each check.
  4. Add up the hours. Gardeners often treat six or more hours of direct sun as full sun, three to six hours as part sun, and less than three as shade.
  5. Repeat when the season changes. The sun's path shifts through the year, and so does the shade from trees, neighbouring buildings and the balcony above yours.

What direction tells you, and what it doesn't

Direction is a useful first clue, not a guarantee. East-facing spaces usually get gentler morning sun. West-facing ones often get strong, hot afternoon sun. In much of India, south-facing spaces tend to receive the longest direct sun through the cooler months, although a high summer sun can be blocked by an overhang. North-facing spaces generally get bright but indirect light, with a little low sun around sunrise and sunset in summer.

Your floor, nearby buildings, awnings, grilles, mesh and tinted glass can all change this. A windowsill behind glass also receives less light than the same sill with the window open.

Matching plants to light

  • Full sun (about six hours or more): fruiting plants such as chillies and tomatoes, marigolds, and tulsi generally do best here.
  • Part sun (about three to six hours): mint, many leafy greens, and coriander in cooler months usually cope well.
  • Bright shade or low light: money plant (pothos), snake plant and spider plant are commonly grown in these conditions.

These are general tendencies. Check the label or ask the nursery before you buy.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing plants for how they look before measuring the light.
  • Judging a spot from a single hour of the day.
  • Putting a sun-lover in the only free corner, even though it stays shaded.
  • Moving a plant from shade straight into strong sun. Do it over several days so the leaves can adjust.

Takeaway. Measure your light before you buy plants. Hours of direct sun in each spot are a better guide than the direction your balcony faces.

Guide 2 · Summer heat

Guide 2

Protecting container plants from Indian summer heat

A plant in a pot is more exposed than one in the ground. The small amount of soil heats up and dries quickly, and walls, railings and floor tiles radiate extra heat back at it. A few small changes can make a real difference in the hottest weeks.

Signs of heat stress

  • Drooping leaves at midday that recover by evening are common and not always a problem.
  • Crisp brown edges, pale or bleached patches, and wilting that continues after watering suggest the plant is struggling.
  • Flowers or young fruit dropping can also follow a very hot spell.

What you can do

  1. Create some distance from heat. Move pots away from hot walls and metal railings, and lift them off the floor on stands or blocks so air can move underneath.
  2. Group pots together. Pots close together shade one another's sides. Put the sturdiest plants on the sunniest side.
  3. Mulch the soil. A layer of dry leaves, coir or straw about two to three centimetres deep slows evaporation. Keep it slightly clear of stems.
  4. Shade the harshest hours. A shade net, light cloth or screen can soften strong afternoon sun. Remember that it also reduces light, so use it when needed rather than all day.
  5. Water at the cooler ends of the day. Early morning is best, with a check in the evening during the hottest weeks. Water slowly until it runs out of the drainage holes.
  6. Check the water itself. Water sitting in an open tank or hose can get very warm in summer. Let it cool before using it.

Pot material matters too. Dark plastic and metal containers can heat up quickly in direct sun. Terracotta lets moisture escape through its walls, so it dries faster, which is a plus in humid weather and a minus in dry heat. Placing a pot inside a slightly larger one can add a little insulation.

Common mistakes

  • Giving small sips of water often, which encourages shallow roots. Water deeply, then wait.
  • Assuming every wilted plant needs water. Check the soil first, because soggy soil can also cause wilting.
  • Feeding heavily during a heat wave. Go gently while plants are stressed.
  • Leaving shade-loving plants on a hot railing.

Takeaway. Protect the roots first. Shade the pot, mulch the soil and water deeply at the cool ends of the day.

Guide 3 · Monsoon

Guide 3

Preparing balcony plants for the monsoon

Rain looks like the easiest season, but containers can become waterlogged, drains can clog and humid air invites fungal problems. A little preparation before the rains arrive goes a long way.

Before the rains

  1. Check every drainage hole. Clear anything blocking them, and make sure each pot actually has holes.
  2. Clear the balcony drain. Fallen leaves and spilled soil can block the outlet, leaving water to pool on the floor.
  3. Raise your pots. Pot feet, bricks or a slatted stand let water escape instead of sitting under the pot.
  4. Loosen packed soil. Soil that has settled hard drains poorly. A well-draining mix, for example one with cocopeat and coarse sand or perlite, helps.
  5. Secure what the wind can move. Tie or fasten hanging pots and tall plants, and trim dead or damaged leaves.

During the rains

  • Water only when the soil needs it. Press a finger into the soil, and skip watering if it still feels damp.
  • Shelter the sensitive ones. Succulents, aloe and some Mediterranean herbs such as rosemary and thyme generally dislike constant wet. Move them under cover if you can.
  • Keep air moving. Avoid crowding pots against each other and against walls.
  • Empty saucers and trays. Standing water can let mosquitoes breed as well as keeping roots too wet.
  • Watch for trouble. Yellowing leaves, black spots, mould on the soil or a sour smell often point to too much moisture.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving full saucers under every pot.
  • Assuming the rain waters everything. A covered or sheltered balcony can stay dry, so check the soil.
  • Packing pots tightly so leaves stay wet for long periods.
  • Feeding heavily when growth is already soft and soggy.

Takeaway. In the monsoon, drainage and airflow matter more than watering. Keep water moving out of the pot and away from your plants.

Guide 4 · Small spaces

Guide 4

Growing more in small spaces

A small balcony does not have to mean a small garden, but it does mean planning. The aim is not to fit in as many pots as possible. It is to give a few plants what they need and leave yourself room to look after them.

Use vertical layers

  • Wall-mounted pots and shelves use space you are not otherwise using.
  • Railing planters work well when they are fixed firmly and sit on the inner side of the railing.
  • Tiered stands and ladder shelves stack several plants in one footprint.
  • Hanging pots free the floor. Check that the hook and the ceiling or bracket can take the weight.
  • Windowsills suit herbs and compact plants, provided the sill gets enough light.

Choose the container for the plant

Herbs and leafy greens can manage in shallower containers. Chillies, tomatoes and other fruiting plants usually need bigger, deeper ones, often around 25 to 30 centimetres across and deep, or more. Treat that as a rough guide and follow the advice on the plant tag or seed packet. Larger pots also hold moisture for longer, which helps in summer.

Plan a simple layout

  1. Measure the space and sketch where the sun falls.
  2. Keep a clear path so you can water, reach and clean.
  3. Put taller plants at the back or side so they don't shade smaller ones.
  4. Group plants with similar watering needs together.
  5. Leave a little gap around pots for airflow and drainage.

Weight and safety

Wet soil is heavy. Lightweight potting mixes and plastic or fabric pots help, and so does avoiding heavy ceramic pots on high shelves. Fix railing and wall planters securely so they cannot fall. Check your housing society's rules, as some restrict planters on the outside of balconies.

Common mistakes

  • Overcrowding until nothing gets light or air.
  • Mixing thirsty plants and drought-tolerant plants in the same pot.
  • Placing unsecured planters on the outer edge.
  • Ignoring the weight of watered soil.

Takeaway. Go up before you go out, choose containers to suit each plant, and always leave room to care for what you grow.

Guide 5 · Beginners

Guide 5

Starting a balcony garden as a beginner

Most first-time gardeners try to do too much too fast. A small, steady start teaches you more than a crowded balcony of plants you can't keep up with, and it is much cheaper if something doesn't work.

A simple first month

  1. Choose one spot. Pick the place you can see and reach easily, and know how much sun it gets.
  2. Pick two or three plants that suit that light, rather than ten.
  3. Use pots with drainage holes. Decorative pots without holes can trap water, so keep plants in a pot with holes and place it inside if you like the look.
  4. Use a potting mix that drains well. Garden soil on its own tends to pack down in pots. Mixes with compost and cocopeat are common choices.
  5. Water by checking the soil. Press a finger two to three centimetres in. Water when it feels dry, and wait when it feels damp.
  6. Observe once a week. A few notes on growth, leaf colour and watering will teach you a lot.
  7. Add more only when this feels easy.

Forgiving plants to consider

  • Bright light: tulsi, marigold and chillies are popular choices. Aloe vera also likes bright light but needs soil that drains freely.
  • Lower light: money plant (pothos), snake plant and spider plant are commonly kept in less sun.
  • One thing to check: some popular plants, including pothos, snake plant and aloe, can be harmful if chewed or eaten by pets or small children. If that applies to your home, check before buying.

Reading what your plant is telling you

Yellow leaves, drooping and long, stretched growth can each have several causes. Stretched, leggy growth often means too little light. Yellowing with constantly wet soil often means too much water. No single sign gives a certain diagnosis, so check the soil, the light and the drainage first, and change one thing at a time.

Common mistakes

  • Buying many plants at once.
  • Watering on a fixed schedule instead of checking the soil.
  • Using pots without drainage holes.
  • Giving up after one loss. A few failures are a normal part of learning.

Takeaway. Start small, match plants to your light, and let the soil tell you when to water.

Questions

Beginner questions, answered honestly

Short, general answers to things new balcony gardeners often wonder about.

Why are my plant's leaves turning yellow?

Yellow leaves have several possible causes, so check before you act. Soil that stays wet with poor drainage is a common one. Dry soil, too little light and a lack of nutrients can also cause it, and older leaves yellow naturally as they age. Look at the soil moisture, the drainage holes and the light, and change one thing at a time.

Should I keep a saucer under every pot?

Saucers protect your floor, but standing water can keep roots too wet and can let mosquitoes breed. If you use saucers, empty them after watering and after rain. Raising pots on small feet or blocks is another way to let water drain away.

Can I grow vegetables on a balcony?

Yes, some of them. Herbs, leafy greens and chillies are popular starting points. Fruiting vegetables such as tomatoes need plenty of direct sun, larger containers and regular watering and feeding. Check the sowing season for your area on the seed packet, and start with a small number of plants.

Do I need special soil or fertiliser?

You don't need anything expensive, but the mix matters. Look for a potting mix that drains well, often a blend with compost and cocopeat. Plants in containers use up nutrients over time, so they usually need feeding as they grow. Follow the instructions on the product label and avoid over-feeding.

What if I am away for a few days in summer?

Water thoroughly before you leave, move pots out of the hottest sun into bright shade, group them together and add mulch to the soil. If you can, ask a neighbour or friend to check on them. Plants differ in how long they can go without water, so for a longer trip, arrange regular help.

Are these guides tested, or specific to my city?

No. They gather general container-gardening principles and explain how they apply to common Indian conditions such as strong summer heat, monsoon rain and small spaces. They are not personal test results or professional advice for your exact city, floor or building. Use them as a starting point, watch your own space, and ask a local nursery which plants suit your area.
