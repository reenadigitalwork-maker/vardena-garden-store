Practical advice that begins with your conditions

Vardena is built around the way Indian homes actually grow: strong summer sun, heavy monsoon rain, limited floor space and plenty of first-time gardeners.

A note on our advice. Vardena guides share general gardening knowledge. They are not presented as personal test results, and your own balcony may behave differently, which is why the guides show you how to observe your space.

How much direct sun does it get?

Read your space

Which way does your balcony face?

Direction is a useful first clue to how much sun your plants will get. These are rules of thumb, not guarantees: nearby buildings, awnings, grilles and the season can all change the picture, so watch your own space for a few days.

Gentle morning light

East-facing

Usually gets softer morning sun and is shaded later in the day, which many leafy plants and herbs find comfortable.

Longest direct sun

South-facing

In much of India it tends to receive the longest direct sun through the cooler months. It can suit sun-loving plants but may need heat protection in summer.

Strong afternoon heat

West-facing

Often brings intense, hot afternoon sun. It can work for sun-lovers, but pots and roots may need shade and careful watering in summer.

Bright, indirect light

North-facing

Usually gets mostly bright but indirect light with little direct sun, so it generally suits shade-tolerant foliage better than fruiting plants.

Questions

Beginner questions, answered honestly

Short, general answers to the questions new balcony gardeners ask most often.

How do I know how much sun my balcony gets?

Check the space at a few points on a clear day, such as mid-morning, noon, mid-afternoon and early evening, and note which spots are in direct sun each time. Add up the hours for each spot. Gardeners often treat six or more hours of direct sun as full sun, three to six hours as part sun, and less than three as shade. Direction, nearby buildings, grilles and the season all change the result, so repeat the check when conditions change.

Can I grow anything on a balcony with little direct sun?

Yes, but choose carefully. Many leafy and foliage plants cope with less direct light than fruiting vegetables such as tomatoes and chillies, which generally need plenty of sun to crop well. Use your brightest spot for the plants that need it most, and match each plant to the light it actually receives.

How do I protect container plants from summer heat?

Keep pots away from hot walls and metal railings where you can, group pots close together so they shade one another, and water in the early morning or evening rather than in the heat of the day. A layer of mulch on the soil surface slows evaporation, and a shade net or cloth can take the edge off the harshest afternoon sun.

What should I check before the monsoon?

Make sure every pot has drainage holes and that the holes are not blocked. Lift pots slightly so water can escape instead of pooling underneath, and move delicate plants out of the heaviest rain if you can. During the rains, water less when the soil stays damp, and watch for yellowing leaves, mould or a sour smell from the soil, which can point to waterlogging.

How often should I water container plants?

There is no single schedule. Press a finger two to three centimetres into the soil. If it feels dry, water slowly until the excess runs out of the drainage holes. If it feels damp, wait. Pot size, plant type, heat, wind and rain all change how quickly soil dries, so small pots in hot, open spots may need checking more often in summer.

Is the advice on Vardena specific to my city?

Not exactly. Vardena guides share general principles for balcony and windowsill growing in Indian homes. Climate varies widely between cities, and even between floors of the same building, so use the guides as a starting point, observe your own space, and ask a local nursery which plants suit your area.
